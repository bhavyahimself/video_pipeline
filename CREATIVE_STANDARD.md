# Viral Video Creative Standard

This is the default creative policy for every Short and long-form video across all channels.

## The required question

Before scripting, ask:

> How can I make this longer and better than the competition?

“Longer” means more complete, valuable, and satisfying—not padded. A Short should be only as long as its strongest story requires.

## Non-negotiables

1. Analyze 3–5 relevant high-performing competitor videos before writing. Record their hook, length, structure, pacing, visuals, narration, payoff, title/thumbnail promise, and gaps.
2. State exactly how this video will be better. Do not start production without a meaningful competition gap.
3. Treat scripting and storyline as 80% of the creative work.
4. Write at a Grade 5–8 reading level. Every line must add information, tension, evidence, or payoff.
5. Open with a question, contradiction, or mystery that gives the viewer something to predict.
6. Tease plausible wrong explanations when accurate: “You may think it was X—or even Y—but both miss the real cause.”
7. Keep the answer unresolved while the story adds useful clues or raises the stakes. Never stall with filler.
8. Fully repay the opening promise. Deliver the reveal quickly and clearly near the end.
9. Do not overexplain, repeat what the visuals already show, mislead the audience, or insult their intelligence.
10. Use subtle viewer participation instead of a blunt subscription command. Good endings include “Did you know before the reveal?”, “Which clue gave it away?”, or “Did your first guess survive the final clue?” Skip the cue if the payoff is stronger alone.

## Daily view-growth loop

Production is not the goal by itself. Each upload must improve the evidence available for the next upload.

1. Compare every Short at like-for-like ages: first hour, 6 hours, 24 hours, and 7 days. Never compare a new Short's early views with an older Short's lifetime views.
2. Record views per hour, viewed-versus-swiped-away, average view duration, average percentage viewed, retention drop-off points, rewatches, engagement per 1,000 views, subscriber gain per 1,000 views, traffic source, and returning viewers when available.
3. Diagnose the largest constraint: topic demand, first-frame clarity, 0–2 second hook, context, stakes, pacing, narration/visual match, voice modulation, captions, payoff, title, or duration.
4. Change only one primary creative variable per experiment when possible so the result teaches something useful.
5. Maintain separate performance, concept, experiment, and lessons logs for every channel.
6. Do not call a format successful because of one outlier. Initial repeatable success requires at least three of the last five Shorts to beat the channel's previous median at the same age while retention or subscriber conversion also improves.

## 80/80 retention target for 30–60 second Shorts

The operating target is now:

- **Stayed to watch: 80% or higher**
- **Average percentage viewed: 80% or higher**

YouTube calculates average percentage viewed from engaged views and their watch time, so these are two separate wins: stop the swipe, then retain the people who stayed. At 80% APV, the approximate average-view-duration targets are 24 seconds for a 30-second Short, 36 seconds for 45 seconds, and 48 seconds for 60 seconds.

These are internal stretch targets, not YouTube rules or guarantees of distribution. Evaluate them at like-for-like ages and do not treat a tiny sample as proof. A creative format becomes reusable only after it approaches the target repeatedly while views, shares, and subscriber conversion also improve.

### First-six-second contract

1. **0.0–0.7 seconds — visual proof:** show the exact danger, trick, decision, identity cue, or failed place in motion. No logo, title card, greeting, or generic establishing shot.
2. **0.7–2.0 seconds — personal question:** give the viewer something consequential to predict about their safety, money, identity, judgment, or future.
3. **2.0–4.0 seconds — close the obvious answer:** introduce a true contradiction or show why the viewer's first explanation may be incomplete.
4. **4.0–6.0 seconds — first evidence reward:** provide a concrete clue, number, transformation, or visual proof. Do not make viewers wait until the ending for all value.

The hook must be truthful. Curiosity is earned by withholding the mechanism while delivering evidence—not by promising an answer the Short does not contain.

### Long-Short retention design

- Place a useful evidence reward every 3–5 seconds.
- Use a meaningful visual reset every 1.5–2.5 seconds.
- Add a mid-video reversal or escalation around 40–55% of runtime.
- Deliver the core reveal around 70–85% of runtime; earlier if the graph shows viewers leave before it.
- After the reveal, use only one consequence, practical takeaway, or loop line. End before energy falls.
- A 45–60 second script must contain more evidence or escalation than a 30-second script—not slower delivery.

## Topic-demand research and scoring

Generate at least 10 distinct concepts before selecting a daily topic. Score each idea out of 100:

- audience demand: 30
- hook and curiosity strength: 25
- exact visual strength and footage availability: 20
- freshness or timeliness: 15
- originality and monetization safety: 10

Research 3–5 relevant winners for the selected concept. Where data is available, distinguish raw lifetime views from age-normalized view velocity. A large old total does not prove that the idea is winning now.

## Approved story shapes

- Hook → supporting hook → scene → stakes → delayed answer → fast payoff → subtle viewer cue
- Hook → rising action → conflict → comeback → rising action → payoff

## Voice standard

Choose the voice, tone, and modulation before editing. Mark changes in pace, energy, emphasis, pitch, and pauses. Build tension with restraint, raise intensity with the stakes, pause before the reveal, and land the payoff confidently.

### Mandatory VEED production rule

VEED is required for the final narration of every narrated Short and long-form video across every channel. Use a high-quality VEED female narrative voice by default, or a deliberately documented channel-specific VEED voice when the channel requires a different persona. The exported VEED narration must be the only primary spoken track in the review master.

Do not use macOS `say`, browser TTS, ElevenLabs, an unapproved local model, or any placeholder voice in a production or review master. Do not reuse a narration track from another project. Start a fresh VEED project for each final video, export the chosen voice immediately, and record the VEED project ID, voice, export filename, and generation date in the package manifest.

VEED must also be used for required voice processing and any added sound design that is part of the production brief. Owned recordings, YouTube Audio Library assets, or clearly licensed effects may supplement VEED only when their source and license are recorded. Reject flat, rushed, muddy, synthetic, duplicated, clipped, or overlapping narration.

Before delivery, listen to the complete master on headphones, phone speakers, and in mono. Confirm that words remain intelligible under music and effects, that sentence-level pace and emotional range change with the story, and that no second voice is audible underneath.

## Sound-hook standard

Sound must carry story information, not merely make the edit louder.

All future productions must follow the full system in `docs/audio_retention_and_sonic_identity_system.md` and include `docs/audio_retention_event_ledger_template.json` in the production package.

1. Open with one subject-specific sound in the first 0.3 seconds: an alarm, impact, UI confirmation, electrical pulse, environmental crack, or other cue that belongs to the exact story.
2. Give each video a restrained sound motif that returns when its central danger, trick, choice, clue, or failure appears. Do not scatter unrelated effects over every cut.
3. Add a purposeful audio reset every 8–15 seconds in Shorts and every 20–40 seconds in long-form: a transition whoosh, evidence click, location ambience change, low impact, or brief music lift.
4. Create contrast before the main payoff. Pull music and ambience close to silence for 0.2–0.6 seconds, then land the reveal with one clean impact or tonal resolve.
5. Keep effects behind the VEED narration. Duck music and ambience during speech, avoid competing frequencies, and reject any mix in which a word becomes harder to understand.
6. Prefer original synthesized effects, owned recordings, YouTube Audio Library assets, or clearly documented commercial/CC0 sources. Record every third-party sound and licence in the source ledger.
7. Never use the same cinematic boom, riser, or notification sound repeatedly enough to feel templated. Relevance beats loudness.
8. Run an audio-event review after rendering: verify that every prominent sound has a story purpose, no effect clips, no voices overlap, and the pre-payoff silence occurs at the intended moment.
9. Use familiar sound meanings such as a tick, lock, cash drawer, paper stamp, radio burst, pressure crack, or subject ambience. Never copy a famous sonic logo, branded interface sound, movie sting, commercial song fragment, meme sound, or competitor effect.
10. Synchronize each featured transient to the exact visual frame where contact, evidence, choice, or change occurs.
11. Limit Shorts to one opening cue, one motif, one midpoint reset, and one payoff cue unless the story clearly earns more.
12. Start music and ambience roughly 18–24 dB below narration during speech, then adjust for phone-speaker intelligibility. Target approximately −16 LUFS integrated and no more than −1.5 dBTP true peak for the final master.
13. Verify the video in mono, at low phone volume, and muted. Important facts cannot exist only in sound.

## Visual and caption standard

- Match each shot to the narration beat and use footage with clear usage rights.
- Begin with motion or a compelling factual image; never use black or static filler.
- Use readable animated captions inside platform safe areas, with selective keyword emphasis.
- Use pacing, shot-scale changes, movement, and sound to reset attention without visual clutter.
- Make the title and thumbnail promise the same payoff the video actually delivers.

### Footage-first evidence rule

When the narration names a game, creator, mechanic, place, object, or event, show that exact subject in motion. For Roblox and other game videos, prioritize owned gameplay capture or clearly licensed footage from the exact experience being discussed: show the avatar, environment, UI action, failure, reward, or mechanic on the frame where it is narrated. Do not substitute generic Roblox footage, a logo, a static screenshot, or unrelated stock gameplay.

For real-world explainers, use authentic licensed/public-domain footage for people, vehicles, weather, wildlife, locations, and consequences whenever it exists. Use original diagrams, maps, or motion graphics only for invisible mechanisms, scale, chronology, or spatial relationships that live footage cannot make clear. If no exact footage is available, rewrite the line or label the visualization honestly; never hide a mismatch under attractive B-roll.

Every explanatory scene should have at least two of these three layers: subject footage or evidence, a clear spatial/causal graphic, and a purposeful camera or text movement. Use a visual reset every 1.5–2.5 seconds in Shorts and every 3–5 seconds in long-form without turning the edit into random zooms.

For high-retention gameplay coverage, use a repeatable sequence: immediate in-game consequence, tight crop on the decision or mechanic, clean UI highlight, wider context shot, then payoff/result. Captions should identify the action or consequence rather than transcribe every word. Use restrained push-ins, punch-outs, cursor/path highlights, and freeze-frame callouts only when they direct attention to evidence.

Never download or reuse another creator's gameplay, facecam, thumbnail, or signature edit without permission. Capture the game yourself, obtain a documented license, or use official press/developer assets with the source and usage terms recorded.

Before editing, create a contract for every narration beat containing:

- narration text
- required visual meaning
- selected asset and exact timestamp
- source and license
- visual-match confidence
- caption emphasis
- motion or transition
- sound direction

The sound direction must name the cue job, cue class, source/license, synchronization point, intended viewer response, repetition count, and mix relationship to narration.

A visually attractive clip is still a failure when it does not directly show, explain, or prove the spoken line. If a strong exact visual cannot be sourced, use an accurate original visualization or rewrite the narration. Do not conceal a mismatch with generic B-roll.

## Mandatory release gates

- **Context gate:** A viewer entering cold must know the named subject, event, mission, company, or place by the end of the second narration beat. Pronouns such as “it,” “this mission,” or “this place” cannot carry the setup alone.
- **Beat-to-visual gate:** Every narration beat must have a written visual requirement before footage is selected. A coffee type, person, location, product, or action named in narration must appear during that beat; generic niche footage is not an acceptable substitute.
- **Audio-continuity gate:** The final assembled file must re-encode narration audio after segment concatenation and pass an automated silence/dropout scan. Reject unexpected interior silence, clicks, clipped words, timing drift, or a narration/music imbalance.
- **Audio-retention gate:** Every featured sound must have a written story job, an exact visual synchronization point, and a verified license. Reject copied signature sounds, lyric-heavy beds under narration, constant alerting, unsynchronized effects, or any mix that loses clarity on a phone speaker.
- **Cold-viewer review:** Review once with no script visible and answer: “What is happening, why does it matter, and did every shot support the words being spoken?” Any unclear answer blocks release.
- **Human approval gate:** No file may be labelled `final_production` until context, audio continuity, visual alignment, caption safe areas, factual accuracy, and copyright records have each been explicitly approved.
- **User review gate:** Every rendered master must be shown to the user for review before YouTube upload. Uploading as private, unlisted, draft, scheduled, or public is prohibited until the user explicitly approves that exact file. A metadata package or an internal QA pass is not approval.
- **VEED provenance gate:** The package must include the VEED project ID, voice name, narration export, sound-design notes, and audio QA result. Missing provenance or a non-VEED primary voice blocks release.
- **Viewer-importance gate:** Before scripting, write the personal question the viewer is trying to answer. If the hook is merely interesting to the producer but does not affect the viewer's safety, money, identity, judgment, status, or understanding of a consequential mystery, strengthen or reject the topic.
- **First-six-second gate:** Review the opener without the title or description. It must show the exact subject in motion, state the personal question, complicate the obvious answer, and deliver one evidence reward by six seconds.
- **80/80 hypothesis gate:** Every production package must state why this opener could reach 80% stayed-to-watch and why the body could reach 80% average percentage viewed. This is a required hypothesis, not a fabricated prediction.

## Required pre-production note

Every production package must contain:

- the competitor benchmark and gap
- the answer to “How is this longer and better?”
- the hook question and final payoff
- the chosen story shape
- the voice and modulation plan
- the beat-by-beat footage, caption, and sound plan
- the factual, copyright, originality, and monetization checks
- one performance hypothesis to test after publishing

No video is review-ready until the hook pays off, the reveal lands quickly, the narration is expressive and clear, and the work is meaningfully stronger than the benchmark.
