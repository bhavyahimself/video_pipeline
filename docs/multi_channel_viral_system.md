# Multi-Channel Viral System

Last updated: 2026-07-26 IST

Purpose: save the reusable script, edit, caption, packaging, and analytics rules that should improve every future Short across the user's channels.

## Core question

Before scripting, ask exactly:

**How can I make this longer and better than the competition?**

Interpret `longer` as more complete and more satisfying, not padded. If the stronger version is shorter, cut it.

## Permanent competitor rule

- Study 3 to 5 relevant competitors for every channel before scripting.
- Record what is working, what looks stale, and what gap they are leaving open.
- Do not clone the competitor's niche straight on. Pick a different but adjacent sub-niche with clearer upside.
- The goal is not `be like X`. The goal is `win in a gap X is not fully serving`.

### Direct source-backed rules

- Shorts planning must center on `how many chose to view`, `shown in feed`, retention, and where viewers find the Short, not views alone.
- Retention graphs must be read after every post and turned into concrete rules for the next edit.
- Mid-video drop-offs usually mean the story took too long to reach the core point.
- It is valid to place the core story beat earlier instead of protecting a slow build.
- YouTube supports up to 3-minute Shorts, but length is not the goal. The story earns the length.
- Vertical composition must protect important elements from UI overlap; YouTube and Google both recommend designing within a safe center area rather than using the edges.

### Benchmark inferences from million-view creators

These are inferences from repeated public-video observation, not direct creator quotes:

- MrBeast-style packaging rule: the first second visually proves the title promise instead of warming up.
- Airrack-style pacing rule: a meaningful visual reset happens about every 1.5 to 2.5 seconds.
- Zack D. Films-style clarity rule: the viewer can explain the premise after one sentence.
- Mark Rober-style payoff rule: the reveal lands cleanly and quickly once enough evidence is on screen.
- Top Shorts usually use sparse emphasis captions, not full transcript captions.
- Top Shorts are usually brighter in the first frame, with stronger local contrast on the subject.
- The narrator's energy rises and falls intentionally. Flat delivery lowers perceived stakes even when the words are good.

## Submodality defaults from now on

### 1. First-frame clarity

- Show the strange thing immediately.
- Use literal contradiction hooks: `That is not...`, `This looks like... but...`, `Everyone thinks this is...`
- No logo, no slow fade, no generic establishing shot.

### 2. Brightness and contrast

- Raise opener exposure and local contrast until the subject reads instantly on a phone in autoplay.
- Avoid murky first frames, haze without separation, or dark footage before the hook is understood.
- If footage is low-contrast, add tasteful contrast shaping instead of over-saturating.

### 3. Captions

- Keep captions above the bottom UI stack and away from the right-side action rail.
- Use 2 to 5 words per caption beat when possible.
- Highlight only the contradiction, stakes word, or payoff word.
- Avoid transcript-style paragraphs.
- Use the internal overlay at `/Users/bshah3/Downloads/Projects/video_pipeline/captions/youtube_shorts_safe_zone_overlay.svg`.

### 4. Narration

- Female VEED narration remains default unless the channel needs a different persona.
- Mark pauses before reveals.
- Increase warmth in setup, urgency in conflict, and certainty at payoff.
- Reject monotone output even if pronunciation is correct.

### 5. Visual pacing

- Reset attention every 1.5 to 2.5 seconds with a new angle, crop, motion beat, text beat, or graphic proof.
- Every spoken line must have directly matching motion footage or motion graphics.
- Do not use static filler while explanation continues.

### 6. Audio retention

- Follow `docs/audio_retention_and_sonic_identity_system.md`.
- Use one quiet, instrumental, story-matched background bed unless deliberate silence is the declared control.
- Use familiar sound meanings, not copied famous recordings: tick for deadline, lock for restriction, cash drawer for money, radio static for communication, crack for failure, data ping for evidence.
- Land important sounds on the exact visual change they describe.
- Give each video one recurring motif and resolve or transform it at the payoff.
- Use a 0.2–0.6 second reduction before the main reveal instead of making every cue louder.
- Limit Shorts to an opening cue, motif, midpoint reset, and payoff cue unless later events add new meaning.
- Record every sound and license in `docs/audio_retention_event_ledger_template.json`.
- Test one audio variable at a time and inspect retention within one second of each cue.

### 7. Script structure

Default to one of these:

1. Hook -> supporting hook -> scene -> stakes -> delayed answer -> fast payoff -> subtle viewer cue
2. Hook -> rising action -> conflict -> comeback -> rising action -> payoff

### 8. Packaging

- Draft 3 title options and 3 cover-frame options before upload.
- The title promise and first frame must match.
- If retention is strong but views are weak, fix topic packaging first.
- If views are weak and stayed-to-watch is weak, fix the first frame and first sentence first.

## Channel-specific carry-forward rules

### Nature Unleashed

- Lean into visual contradiction and real spectacle.
- Use official science footage or public-domain disaster footage with one clean explanation path.
- Avoid poetic titles that hide the literal visual.
- The viewer's important needs are personal safety, warning time, geographic relevance, and understanding whether astonishing footage is real.
- Prioritize questions such as:
  - `Could this happen where you live?`
  - `How much warning would you actually have?`
  - `What would reach you first—the wave, ash, heat, or shock wave?`
  - `Why did the disaster everyone expected not happen?`
  - `What is the one sign people misunderstand before this starts?`
  - `How can something thousands of kilometres away still affect you?`
- Hook pattern: show the real event first, ask the personal consequence, then overturn the obvious scientific guess.

### One Decision That Changed History

- Retention can be high even when reach is low. That usually means the topic or title is too niche or too softly packaged.
- Put the decision in the first line, then the giant consequence.
- The viewer's important needs are moral prediction, hidden context, preventable consequences, and whether they would have made the same choice.
- Prioritize questions such as:
  - `Would you have made the decision that nearly changed your world?`
  - `How close did one ordinary person come to preventing—or causing—disaster?`
  - `What warning did the people in charge ignore?`
  - `Why did the safer-looking choice become the dangerous one?`
  - `What did the decision-maker know that the public did not?`
  - `Which single hesitation changed what happened next?`
- Hook pattern: name the person, place, and decision immediately; make the viewer choose; then reveal the missing information that changes the choice.

### Designed To Trick You

- The trick must be visible in frame one.
- Use screen recordings, store walkthroughs, or on-object closeups instead of abstract narration.
- End with a practical defense tip.
- The viewer's important needs are protecting money, time, privacy, and freedom of choice.
- Prioritize questions such as:
  - `Which option are they quietly steering you toward?`
  - `How much is this “free” choice really costing you?`
  - `What changes on this screen when you try to say no?`
  - `Which price exists only to make another price look smart?`
  - `What does this app know will make you keep scrolling?`
  - `What can you do in five seconds to break the trick?`
- Hook pattern: let the viewer make a choice on screen, expose the hidden comparison or friction, then give a practical defense.

### Why This Place Failed

- Generic category hooks underperform. Named places or brands are stronger.
- The `final nail` must arrive fast and be concrete.
- The viewer's important needs are discovering the hidden fatal flaw, understanding whether it could happen again, and seeing what was lost or remains today.
- Prioritize questions such as:
  - `What fatal flaw was hidden while this place looked successful?`
  - `Could the same mistake destroy a place like yours?`
  - `Who saw the collapse coming—and why were they ignored?`
  - `What was the last normal day here?`
  - `Why could money not save this place?`
  - `Can it still be fixed, or has it passed the point of return?`
- Hook pattern: show the place at its peak and after failure in the first two seconds, state the impossible contradiction, then trace one causal mechanism.

### What Your X Says About You

- The viewer must identify themselves within the first two options.
- Better-performing versions should use objects people see every day and emotionally identify with.
- The viewer's important needs are self-recognition, social comparison, reassurance, and one useful insight about a daily habit. Keep claims playful and evidence-bounded; never present entertainment as diagnosis.
- Prioritize questions such as:
  - `Which group are you in before the answer appears?`
  - `Is your habit normal—or are you the outlier?`
  - `What does this tiny choice reveal about how you handle stress?`
  - `What would someone correctly guess about you from this object?`
  - `Which version of you appears when you are tired or rushed?`
  - `Does your first guess about yourself survive the final clue?`
- Hook pattern: make the viewer count, choose, or classify themselves immediately; reveal recognisable groups quickly; then land one surprising but non-diagnostic insight.

## Cross-channel 80/80 operating rules

- The first-frame question must matter to the viewer, not merely to the subject.
- Use `you` only when the consequence is real and specific; avoid empty phrases such as `you won't believe`.
- Make viewers choose between two plausible answers before supplying the missing clue.
- Give a micro-payoff by six seconds, a stronger reward near the midpoint, and the promised reveal near the end.
- Test one hook variable at a time: first visual, first sentence, personal stake, or wrong-answer tease.
- Review at first hour, 6 hours, 24 hours, and 7 days. Record stayed-to-watch, average percentage viewed, average view duration, shares, comments, subscribers per 1,000 engaged views, and the largest retention drop.
- When stayed-to-watch is below 80%, rewrite the first frame and first two sentences before changing the ending.
- When stayed-to-watch is at least 80% but APV is below 80%, keep the premise and rebuild context density, evidence spacing, visual resets, and payoff timing.
- When both exceed 80% at a meaningful sample, produce several adjacent stories using the same viewer promise while preserving originality.

### Star Scoop Central

- Use one clean celebrity angle per Short, not a pile of gossip fragments.
- If reliable footage is limited, use licensed stills plus animated headline proof instead of questionable clips.

### TaylorSwiftDaily

- Only use timely claims backed by reputable current sources.
- Fastest reliable angle: one new fact, one implication, one payoff.

### Mathematics Simply Explained

- Open with the impossible-looking result.
- Use one proof path only.
- The final answer should feel like a reveal, not a lecture.

## Analytics decision tree

- Weak views + weak `chose to view`: fix the first frame and title promise.
- Strong `chose to view` + weak avg viewed: cut setup, increase motion, move payoff earlier.
- Strong retention + weak reach: upgrade topic demand and packaging.
- Strong reach + weak engagement: the hook won but the body lost.
- Search share rising: lean harder into literal, searchable phrasing.

## Stored hypotheses to keep testing

- Literal contradiction hooks outperform poetic or atmospheric hooks on science and explainers.
- Sparse emphasis captions outperform transcript captions on Shorts.
- Brighter, higher-contrast openers increase `chose to view`.
- Named entities outperform generic categories for history, business, and failure channels.
- Viewer-participation endings outperform blunt subscribe asks.

## Current baseline and improvement ladder

- current stayed-to-watch range: 15.8–45.1%

- current latest-video APV range where available: about 32–44%, except prior Nature examples that reached higher

This means the system is getting initial feed tests but is not yet earning a large second distribution wave.

For the next production cycle:

1. First make 50% stayed-to-watch the minimum practical milestone for every channel.
2. Move 25–35 second Shorts above 60% APV.
3. Scale only the concepts that repeat those gains, then progress toward 70% and the 80/80 stretch target.
4. Record all data at like-for-like ages and change one primary creative variable per experiment.
